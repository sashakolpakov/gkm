\begin{abstract}
We compare mechanical search and a language model as methods for building
reusable programs from small token-processing machines. An initial register
transducer benchmark examines which elementary operations suffice for short
sequence tasks. Later experiments hold those operations fixed within each
comparison and allow richer compositions, recursive control and procedure
interfaces. Both proposers use the same graph pushout construction and execution
checks. Mechanical search is faster on the simpler composition families. On a
harder recursive task, the language model solves acquisition and a subsequent
task on both tested data seeds; the mechanical baseline times out on both
acquisitions after twenty minutes each. One model transition reuses an unchanged
helper. The other extends its interface, with the old program recovered exactly
by fixing the new argument. A separate retention experiment shows that two
earlier interface extensions enable cheaper later solutions. The original
benchmark uses an explicit loss-plus-size objective; the growth experiments
instead accept the first proposal that passes their checks and record size
afterwards. The results distinguish finding a useful interface from searching
its bindings, without establishing a general advantage of language models over
mechanical synthesis.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Building a small program and building a useful library are different problems.
A program may solve its present task yet fix a choice that the next task needs
to vary. Conversely, a larger helper may earn its cost when later programs can
call it with different arguments. We study this distinction using transducers
over opaque tokens: programs can move, copy and compare tokens, but cannot
refer to their literal identities.

The experiments address two questions. First, which elementary operations are
needed for short sequence transformations? Second, once those operations are
available, how can a solver discover reusable control and interfaces? The first
study uses evolutionary search with a loss-plus-size objective. The later
studies compare mechanical construction with proposals from
\texttt{gpt-5.6-sol}, a large language model (LLM). The two methods share the
same acceptance checks and retain complete successful additions to their
libraries.

Description size connects the studies, but plays different roles in them.
The original objective follows the preference for concise explanations found
in minimum description length and loss--complexity methods
\cite{rissanen1978,losscomplexity}. The later studies ask whether reuse emerges
from program organisation and proposal preferences, without ranking successful
programs by a scalar score. All reported sizes are counts in a chosen syntax,
not measurements of Kolmogorov complexity \cite{kolmogorov1965,live2008}.

\section{The initial register-transducer benchmark}
\label{sec:register}

\subsection{Representation and selection}

A transducer maps a control state and an observation to a short instruction
sequence and a next state. Observations report a token, the end of input, or,
where permitted, the beginning of input and equality with stored registers.
A missing rule halts execution. The tested instruction sets are:
\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabular}{ll}
\toprule
Set & Available operations \\
\midrule
\texttt{stream} & Move right, emit current token, halt \\
\texttt{register} & Stream operations plus register storage and emission \\
\texttt{compare} & Register operations plus token/register equality tests \\
\texttt{bidirectional} & Stream operations plus move left and beginning-of-input test \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{center}
These are supplied alternatives, not capabilities discovered by the search.
In particular, bidirectional motion is a separate branch rather than an
extension of the register sets.

Evolution ranks candidates by
\begin{equation}
 F_\lambda = R + \lambda C,
 \qquad C=\sum_{r\in\mathcal R}(1+|a_r|),
 \label{eq:fe}
\end{equation}
where $R$ is mean normalised edit distance between output and target,
$\mathcal R$ is the stored rule map, and $|a_r|$ is the number of instructions
in rule $r$. Unused rules still count. Available registers, unused states and
the instruction vocabulary have no separate charge. Thus $C$ discourages
extra code; it does not choose the primitive set or price all machine resources.

The runner uses four values of $\lambda$ between zero and 0.01, or 0.006 for
the register rotation and bidirectional reversal conditions. It takes the final
training winner from each run, forms their validation loss--size Pareto set,
and chooses the smallest candidate within 0.075 of the best validation loss.
Lower validation loss breaks a size tie. Despite the function name
\texttt{validation\_elbow}, this is a tolerance rule, not an estimated geometric
knee or a global minimum over programs.

Each condition uses one fixed data seed, with ten training, six validation and
six hidden examples. Token pools are disjoint and contain ten symbols each.
The search budgets vary by condition. Evaluation is capped at 32 primitive
instructions; its exact-match score compares the resulting output with the
target without requiring that the program halted. This older metric must be
distinguished from the normal return and complete input consumption required
in the later procedure experiments.

On 4 October 2026 we repeated all ten conditions with the original seeds,
budgets and selection code. Hidden accuracy, loss and size matched every
reported row. The new archive contains all forty lambda winners, their
generation histories, selected programs and execution records. This is a
documented reproduction, not a recovered copy of the original run.

The representation explains some failures independently of search. Without
registers or a way to move left, a program cannot recover a token after passing
it. It therefore cannot swap an arbitrary pair of distinct opaque tokens.
Without an equality observation, control follows the same path for equal and
unequal pairs of the same length, so it cannot implement the required change
in output length for deduplication. These arguments concern these instruction
sets, not a general necessity for registers in every implementation.

The successful motifs reported with the matrix are simple: store the first
token while emitting the second, or scan to the right end and emit while
moving left. The reversal loop suggests a solution for longer inputs when
execution limits are raised; the six length-five tests do not by themselves
establish that claim. Nor does the size of a selected solver demonstrate that
it is minimal. This benchmark motivates the later study of shared programs,
but provides no evidence of library growth on its own.

\section{Discussion}

The experiments separate two practical difficulties. Once a useful interface
exists, mechanical binding search is often enough: it was fast in the simpler
comparisons and supplied every transfer in the retention assay. Discovering
new recursive control was harder for the tested search policy. The LLM reached
further there, but sometimes overfit the examples or chose an interface that
had to be extended on the next task.

The categorical construction is common to both methods. The LLM's flexibility
lies in proposing the attaching program, not in passing a weaker verifier.
Retaining a successful attachment changes the library from which the next
proposal is built. Reuse preferences can therefore influence the sequence of
retained programs even though no scalar complexity score decides admission.
We did not ablate those preferences, and the observed size drops do not prove
that they alone caused reuse or that free energy was minimised.

The distinction between direct reuse and interface extension matters here.
A small new call is strong evidence of reuse when the old helper is unchanged,
executes and is necessary. A costly extension can also be productive: the
retention assay shows later savings that an immediate size-drop criterion would
miss. Neither outcome follows merely from making a mathematically valid
attachment.

These are bounded experiments with supplied languages, types, memory conventions
and curricula. The recursive interface studies also start with three supplied
source programs. The mechanical baseline searches only a subset of the accepted
language; its timeouts do not rule out better mechanical algorithms. The model
has pretrained knowledge and remote compute, so equal deadlines are not equal
compute budgets. Finally, the data seeds share task rules, and many executions
reuse the same learned program. They support the reported constructions, not a
population-wide success rate. Discovering useful interfaces across unrelated
tasks, or combining several independently learned parsers, remains untested.
